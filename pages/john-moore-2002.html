Rally 27 (2002): Living on Twenty Pence by John Moore

Living on Twenty Pence

I could begin with the flaky paint, the leaks, the dampness and a hundred other problems. But I wont, I will start with the fun, the pleasure and the wonderful times we had on board the first proper boat we owned.

It all started one wet windy day in Kilgarvan. While walking past the green shed I saw Ritchie O’Donoghue sitting smoking his pipe and looking out across the Lake. I stopped for a chat and he told me he was working on his new boat. He introduced me to Twenty Pence and I was smitten at once. Over the next couple of weeks I watched as he lavished all his care and attention on her in his own slow and methodical way. At that time she appeared to me to be a ship.

Twenty Pence is an Eventide 24, which was designed by Maurice Griffiths after the war. He was the Editor of Yachting Monthly and a life long advocate of shallow draft creek crawlers. He designed it to be built by any reasonably skilled amateur from readily available materials. It was designed for a family of four to sail safely and with reasonable performance. It is said you can dip the cross trees in the water and she will self-right, a theory I never put to the test. One of the very first Eventides built was by a British diplomat living in Singapore. He wrote to Maurice Griffiths and told him he planned to sail it home to Britain and asked what modifications he should make before embarking on such a voyage, "Just launch it and go" was the reply. The Diplomat and a friend did in fact sail it across the Indian Ocean through a hurricane or a "trout breeze" as Mr Boyle would say and arrived safely in Britain.

(This bit of the story always makes me cringe a little when I think of the days on the Rally that I sat in port because it was too windy!!)

Ritchie O'Donoghue then sadly got ill and decided that it was time to hang up his sailing boots and after some gentle negotiations a deal was struck and Twenty Pence became ours.

Our maiden voyage was on a Saturday morning and Terryglass was to be the destination. Ritchie kindly lent us his fishing boat as a tender for the first sea trials and with both elation and fear we got under way. We guided our ship towards Gortmore, "This is The Life," we thought as we motored across the mirror flat lake. As we approached Gortmore I noticed an abandoned lake boat off the aft quarter and said to Anne that we should probably go and rescue it before someone stole it or the wind got up and damaged it. We turned and headed for the fishing boat just as an Emerald Star cruiser came into view heading for it too. I put on a little more power from our Stuart Turner engine and just about beat the cruiser to the abandoned boat. As I came along side I thought it looked familiar. As I took hold of the painter I suddenly realised that it was Ritchie's treasured fishing boat that I had, in my daze of glory and emotion, lost some time before without even noticing.

Twenty Pence was very hard on engines. We went through three in seven years, the final one being a four stroke outboard which looked ungainly but performed beautifully throughout its life.

I read somewhere that the amount of pleasure to be had on a boat goes up directly in proportion as the size of the boat goes down. This I would have to agree with even though we now have an enormous twenty-seven footer that we have difficulty finding our way around on and we wonder what on earth we will put in all the lockers and cupboards! I am sure in time they, as on other boats I have been on, will be jammed to capacity with essential odds and ends that will never see the light of day again.

On our new ship I now have SYSTEMS. Everyone else always had systems, we used to have them too but I threw them all overboard. We had nothing that couldn't be started with a match and turned off with a pufffffffff.... a small part of me longs for simpler times but I think the crew has had enough of the simple life.

I am delighted that Twenty Pence remains in the Kilgarvan Family and is now in the loving hands of Colin Coady and his lovely girlfriend Bernie. I envy their youth and enthusiasm as they begin their sentence; we had seven years with time off for good behaviour. Even though I have now joined the plastic boat fraternity I will always cast a longing glance towards boats like Twenty Pence and other wooden wonders but I am sure when I remember the damp, wet, musty, stale, soggy timber that often brushed my cheek as I slept I will soon look at my new ship with more than a little affection.

John Moore
