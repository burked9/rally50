Rally Magazine 26 (2001): Is St. Patrick Really Dead?

Is St. Patrick Really Dead?

As all of you know St. Patrick did die fado fado; yet since 15 odd years ago, when the now six foot odd Daniel Becker was seven or eight years old, the news of our patron saint's demise came as quite a shock! - Some days before the 17th March Daniel's teacher was telling his class the history of St. Patrick. Young Daniel came home to Jennie, his mom, in floods of tears, crying because he had learnt that St. Patrick was dead! Jeannie said "I know he is dead", to which Daniel replied, "but what about poor Mrs. St. Patrick?" - The poor child was convinced that I had died, and that my wife Anna was left all alone! Thus I guess that over the years boats in general and barges in particular tend to be inextricably associated with their owners.

In my case I bought the St. Patrick to save my marriage! - yes its true. Anna my long-suffering wife (48 years now!), would get sea sick in a bath tub, whereas I love sailing at sea. Surely a barge on the Shannon would be the ideal compromise, and would hold the marriage together! Some 30 years ago Irish River Floatels had gone into liquidation, and their four barges; the St. Patrick, the St. James the Linquenda and the Athlone Castle were offered for sale by the liquidator at Killaloe. Like Caesar, I came, I saw and was conquered by; the St. Patrick. Fortune shone on the Kerrigan Family in the person of the late Anthony Donoghue, St. Patrick's former skipper. He and I bonded immediately as he introduced me to the vagaries of this 99 ton monster. Bicycle clips securely fastened I sallied forth at the enormous wheel of this leviathan with Anthony by my side. As I became more familiar with this floating apartment block, which would sail in a beam wind, I became more daring and of course more foolhardy, like the occasion on the Upper Shannon Rally when I took the barge up The Lecarrow Canal only to find that I had no space to turn at the end. So, I had to spend over two hours reversing the 1.5 miles back to Lough Ree!

Some years later on a Lough Derg Rally I took her up the Scarriff River, turned her with lots of help in Scarriff Harbour, but lost her wheel house; it got caught under a tree on the one of the bends near Tuamgraney. What a pity that she cannot be hinged amidships.

A guy whom I met in Annapolis when I sailed to the U.S. for the 1976 Bicentenary Celebrations came visiting Ireland later and stayed a few days with us on the St. Patrick. When he returned home he 'phoned and told me about a book titled "Time Enough" which he had got in his local library. Thus an account of a cruise on the Shannon on the St. Patrick by a party of Americans (and written by one of them). When I asked Anthony Donoghue if he know of this book his reply was "I thought that that bloody book was long since out of print". When my American friend sent me the library copy I understood the reason for Anthony's remark: he figures prominently in the book - The Yanks, would enquire at what time they were leaving a particular place, and Anthony would reply in his laid back fashion "time enough"; or the crew would say that Anthony had gone for a 'jar' and the Americans would wonder what sort of an earthenware vessel he would return with! - I still have and treasure this one remaining copy of "time enough".

According to the Bill of Sale etc. which came with the vessel she was built in the U.K. in 1935, and gloried in the name of "Avon King". CIE bought the St. Patrick, her sister ship (the St. Brigid) plus the St. James in 1945. All three vessels were used as Shannon Tugs, towing canal barges and carrying 80 tons of Guinness or cement. The 75 H.P. Bolinder engine on the Patrick was replaced by a 120 H.P. Perkins. The first two locks and one of the dry docks at Shannon Harbour were lengthened by 30 feet to accommodate these vessels. I imagine that this sizeable building operation cost a lot more than was paid for the barges in the first place! I have used this facility to dry dock several times, and all at the nominal cost of £5 per day.

The next chapter in the history of the St. Patrick began when commercial traffic on the Shannon ceased in 1959 and C&E replaced it with rail and road transport. Both the St. Patrick and the St. James were then bought and tastefully converted to flotels by a very far seeing and imaginative English man. Ronald Kersley and his wife established "Irish River Floatels" - Operating from a base at Athlone with a crew of three on each vessel (A Captain, a hostess and a Cook) they offered one or two week cruises. One week north to Carrick-on-Shannon and a second week from Athlone to Killaloe and return. Mr. Kersley would collect his customers in Dublin or Shannon in his minibus. They were welcomed on board to a sherry reception by the crews of each vessel resplendent in their uniforms.

On Wednesday or Thursday of each week Mr. Kersley would meet the boats and take his passengers on a day tour by minibus; to the Glen of Aherlow on the Southern leg. The attached copies of old itineraries give some idea of the luxury and value offered. Mr. Kersley's enterprise went so well that he subsequently bought and converted two Dutch barges - the Linquenda and the Villeestrom (now the Arklow Castle). Sadly after 10 years of operation (1960 to 1970) this business was badly hit by the emergency troubles in Northern Ireland; the customer base being mainly elderly English people. Irish River Floatels went into liquidation and the four barges were tied up for two years at Killaloe.

About this time, for reasons already mentioned, it was decided that the Kerrigan family needed a barge badly and so ourselves and the St. Patrick became an item, 28 years ago. In the intervening years our floating home has brought tremendous joy and pleasure to our extended family and many friends. Most of the Kerrigan's third generation (our grandchildren) are now involved and want to escape the fleshpots of Dublin, and spend all of their holidays on board. We have entered a couple of Shannon Rallies and have taken part in almost all of the Lough Derg Rallies since. We can accommodate twelve comfortable in four single and four double cabins, but have often had up to twenty crew on board. The St. Patrick has over the years seen many wild and memorable parties; Asgard Support Group 'Meetings', Ski Club weekends; scuba dive get togethers, hill walking weekends in the Slieve Blooms in Winter and back again to the Barge for well earned drum ups. We have also had Mass on board on a number of occasions and countless birthday and anniversary parties. The attractive aspect of these soirees being the fact that after several years you just slide down the stairs and go to bed. When moored in various harbours we have often been asked if we serve afternoon teas, or do trips on the lake!

Not being particularly mechanically minded I have been fortunate in my many gifted friends who helped keep the St. Patrick in good nick - people like the late Terence Mallogh who came on several rallies with us, the late Len Breewood, Marine Surveyor and Cordon Blue Chef extradordinaire. Alan Geraghty to whom any mechanical problems is just a mere challenge, Pat Flynn, a genius with electrics, Nick Coady who when he was Bank Manager in Borrisokane helped us find our Island home at Kilgarvan. Chris McNally, Eamon Egan and so many others without all of whom the St. Patrick would probably not still be afloat. Lastly and by no means least Lady Anna Kerrigan who hates cleaning St. Patrick's many windows, but who loves to fuss over and spoil all of the crew.

By way of conclusion I now officially refute Rory Stephen's remarks at last years Rally dinner, and state that although the real St. Patrick may be dead, the barge St. Patrick is by no means dead and please God will see many more rallies.

Written by Seamus Kerrigan for Rally Magazine 26 (2001)
