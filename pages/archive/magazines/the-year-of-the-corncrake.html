The Year of the Corncrake - 1991

This article was extracted from the Rally 16 brochure in 1991.

Corncrakes are rarely seen once they arrive from their long migration because they seek the cover of long vegetation and are reluctant to fly. The harsh 'crek crek' repetitive call of the male corncrake, heard mostly at night, is often the only indication that the bird is present. Corncrakes have short stumpy tails and trail their legs when flying. Both sexes are alike, basically brown with grey on the head and neck, chestnut on the wings and black streaks on the back.

Corncrakes spend the winter in marsh and long grassland in Africa. When they arrive in April they use what cover is available mostly on agricultural land, rushy pastures, reeds and marshy areas and tall vegetation like nettles and hogweed. They eat slugs, snails, worms, beetles, flies and caterpillars.

Breeding starts about mid-May. They nest on the ground and normally lay two clutches of eggs. Incubation takes about 19 days and the chicks are led away from the nest a few hours after hatching by the female. She leaves them after about two weeks and lays another clutch.

Unfortunately the numbers of corncrakes in Ireland have declined in recent decades. This was first noted in the east around 1900. By 1970 they were rare in the east and south of the country. Most people believe that changes in farming practices, particularly silage making, are responsible. Other factors that have contributed to the decline are early meadow cutting, increased drainage of marshy land and increased sheep production. The Shannon Callows (Athlone to Portumna) is the only area where the Corncrake population appears to remain stable. So all you Rally-goers who venture as far as Meelick for the unofficial start, listen for the late night 'crek crek' on your way home from the local.

The Irish Wildbird Conservancy is a national voluntary organisation for the conservation of wild birds and their habitats. They plan to buy land where Corncrakes breed on the Shannon Callows, to protect these birds and to provide a model of management for Corncrakes.
