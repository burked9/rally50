Title: The Fish of the Shannon
Date: 2024-02-15

*This article was extracted from the Rally 17 magazine.*

**SALMON**
*(Salmo salar). Ir. Bradán; Fr. Saumon; Ge. Lachs; Du. Zalm; It. Salmone de reno; Sp. Salmón.*

More important perhaps from a general interest point of view are the salmon and the trout, both of ancient lineage and closely related. The salmon supports a commercial fishery in the estuary and in the upper tidal waters, as well as a rod fishery. Today, they are rarely caught in the upper reaches.

Needless to say, the best specimens will be found in the tidal waters. By the time Boyle Water is reached, the fish will have been in fresh water a long time and their general condition will have deteriorated. When salmon come up river to spawn they cease feeding and literally wear themselves out in the search for the right place to breed. When this is found, the female hollows out a shallow trough with her body and fins and lays her eggs, which are fertilised by the male. Their one purpose accomplished, both male and female seem to lose all interest in life and drift listlessly down-river to the sea and to death. It is generally supposed that the salmon lives to spawn only once, though this has been denied by some experts.

**BROWN TROUT**
*(Salmo trutta). Ir. Breac; Fr. Truite; Ge. Forelle; Du. Forel; It. Trota; Sp. Trucha.*

The trout, a close relative to the salmon, would appear to be more fortunate or less delicate.

It does not die after breeding but lives to spawn three, or possibly, four times. Brown trout reach large dimensions in the limestone reaches of the main river and in its lakes and tributaries.

**POLLAN**
*(Coregonus albula).*

An interesting Irish species of white-fish, known in this country as pollan, is found in the Shannon lakes, particularly Lough Derg and Lough Ree. They are seldom seen and are only captured occasionally in the nets at the eel weirs.

**EEL**
*(Anguilla anguilla). Ir. Eascon; Fr. Anguille; Ge. Aal; Du. Paling; It. Anguilla; Sp. Anguila.*

One fish, which is rarely mentioned — and may well be despised by casual anglers — is the eel. Yet this palatable and interesting fish is plentiful in all parts of the Shannon system and is captured by baited long lines and traps. Eels inhabit not only rivers and lakes, but also brooks and isolated ponds, sometimes remote from the river or its tributary streams.

Towards the autumn some eels cease feeding and become silvery; the snout also becomes sharp and the eyes larger. These silver eels descend the river to the sea and they travel countless miles across the Atlantic to breed in the sea, south east of Bermuda and then, with that almost ritual finality of sub-rational nature, to die.

**LAMPREY**
*(Petromyzon marinus). Ir. Loimpre; Fr. Lamproie marine; Ge. Meerneunauge; Du. Zeeprik; It. Lampreda di mare; Sp. Lamprea de mar.*

Three species of lamprey, the large marine lamprey, the smaller river lamprey and the stream lamprey, or lampern, frequent the River Shannon. The first species spawns in April or May in the lower part of the main river; the second may run up into Lough Derg. The lampern is distributed through even the smallest streams. The flesh of the lamprey is considered a delicacy in parts of Europe, but is very indigestible. Consider the fate of Henry I, who died after eating a 'surfeit of lampreys'!

The Mysis relicta, the Irish pollan and the char are all relict Arctic species, locked in the Shannon lakes.

Mention should also be made of the smelt, which spawns in the tidal waters in the month of April or May.

**BREAM**
*(Abramis brama). Ir. Bréan; Fr. Brême; Ge. Blei, Brachsen; Du. Brasem; It. Brama.*

A large flat fish growing to as much as 12 lbs. in weight. The meadows stretch below Athlone is probably the best bream fishery in Europe but bream are to be found all along the Shannon system. Rudd/bream and roach/bream hybrids may also be found.

**PIKE**
*PIKE (Esox lucius). Ir. Liús; Fr. Brochet; Ge. Hecht; Du. Snoek; It. Luccio; Sp. Lucio.*

Pike are found everywhere on the Shannon and live on other fish. They have a torpedo shaped body and are a very large fish growing to as much as 60 lbs. in weight.

**PERCH**
*(Perca fluviatilis). Ir. Péirse; Fr. Perche; Ge. Baarsch; Du. Baars; It. Pesce Persico; Sp. Perca.*

Also found everywhere, these are a small, edible fish, growing to about 6 lbs. maximum.

**ROACH**
*(Rutilus rutilus). Ir. —; Fr. Gardon; Ge. Plötze, Rotauge; Du. Blankvoorn; It. Triotto; Sp. Bermejuela.*

Roach, which have been introduced from the Erne by continental anglers using them as live bait, are spreading all over the Shannon. They are a voracious fish devouring vegetation and taking over from the natural species. Rudd/roach and rudd/bream hybrids are possible.

**RUDD**
*(Scardinius erythrophthalmus). Ir. Tallaig or Róiste; Fr. Rotengle; Ge. Rotfeder; Du. Ruisvoorn; It. Scardola.*

Very plentiful, and in some areas mainly Garnafeilagh Lough north of Athlone, a rudd/bream hybrid is found.

**TENCH**
*(Tinca tinca). Fr. Tanche; Ge. Schleie; Du. Zeelt; It. Tinca; Sp. Tenca.*

A thick set fish growing to about 10 lbs. Originally from Asia and Siberia, it thrives in weedy ponds and is good to eat.

**'TIDDLERS'**
Other fishes in the Shannon system include minnows, stickle-backs (the 'tiddlers', beloved of the schoolboy with a jam-jar), loaches and gudgeon and these four small species are widely spread.
