Most navigators of Lough Derg are familiar with the beauty of its many islands, wooded shores and promontories, the gap in the Tipperary mountains which the Devil, in a bad mood, bit out and spat into Lough Derg creating Islandmore, the great rounded mountain of Tountinna (where Fintan slept securely under the waters of the Deluge) and the majestic round tower on the ruined monastic site of Holy Island. Yet it is only when one dares to venture into one of the myriad inlets that the innermost secrets and history of this great lake are revealed.

One such inlet is Church Bay, situated mid-way between Mountshannon and Williamstown. Hare Island and Island Hobart guard the entrance to this tranquil bay, which gets its name from a little known monastic site at the rear of the thatched cottages. This is Clonrush which was a link in the chain of Early Christian Ecclesiastical foundations which were once numerous along the Shannon. The Parish of Whitegate from earliest times was called Clonrush, a name with significant ecclesiastical connotations i.e. Clonard, Clonmacnoise, Clonfert, etc. Landlord interference created the meaningless name of WHITEGATE in the early 19th century.

Clonrush monastic site now embraces about three quarters of an acre and three ruins.

1. St. Colman's cell or oratory is located near the entrance gate. It is a small oblong building 10 ft. 4 inches by 8 feet 6 inches. It is 8 ft. high and has a stone and mortar roof. The inside is plastered using a rare technique known as plank shuttering. On the west side there is a Gothic limestone doorway which was added as part of a re-construction job in the 16th century. Behind this doorway can be traced the remains of the original which was Trabeated i.e. 28" wide at the top and 24" at the bottom. On the east gable there is a small rectangular window 12" high and 6" wide. It is said that the patron saint of the Parish, St. Colman, could kneel inside this window and hear Mass being celebrated in Rome. The imprint of his knees could be seen on a stone. This legendary stone probably refers to a double Bullaun stone, now broken, which can still be seen here. The incredible acoustics within the building probably accounts for this tall tale. In the 18th century the building was used as a confessional, a purpose to which it was ideally suited. Later it was known as Bolton's house. Bolton was a small tenant farmer who is supposed to have raised pigs within this building. In modern times it was known as the skull house because of the careless and abominable practise of depositing bones etc. there by grave-diggers. The building has now been completely renovated by a local Heritage Group which was the forerunner of the East Clare Heritage Company.
2. At the Western end of the graveyard are the remains of an ancient church - the fabric of which measures 48 feet 6 inches by 16 feet 6 inches. Re-construction work on this site was done by the East Clare Heritage Company. This building served as the Parish church from the 12th century until about 1750 when it was used as a school.

Only the East gable and parts of the North and South walls survive. On the inside of the East gable can be seen a typical 12 century undecorated Romanesque window. The outside has been altered to include a beautiful 16 century cut limestone window with two lights. Close examination of this gable will reveal that at some stage the roof was raised about two feet. Another building ran parallel with this church for half its length. Only the archway of chiselled stone and part of the foundation remains. An ancient custom, which is still adhered to, is that all burials in Clonrush go through this arch. This custom is very ancient and has descended from pagan to Christian times. Celtic peoples always approached a holy location from the North side and were accustomed to turn sunwise, i.e. from left to right in the performance of certain rites, particularly with regard to burials. From left to right is considered lucky, from the opposite direction, unlucky.

3. In the centre of the cemetery is another old ruin measuring 28 feet by 15 feet. This is known as Tigh na mBrathar i.e. the house of the Friars. Local tradition states that it was the priests house. A slab in this ruin records the grave of John A. Burke.

John Anthony wrote the Epitaph himself six years before he eventually died. He was allowed to rest in peace until 1933 when enquiries were being made for the next of kin of his reputed illigitimate son Martin Edward Burke who died in Darlinhurst, Australia in apparent poverty — though actually possessed of £50,000. Seven hundred Burkes immediately claimed kinship, and a swarm of legal representatives from all parts of the world descended on John Anthony's grave, photographed the inscription and anticipated the judgement of God by holding a human judgement on his supposed moral lapses. The saga has continued to the present day and the legend of John Anthony grows more complex yearly as more evidence of his life and times comes to light. Both Protestant and Catholic are buried in Clonrush.

"Beneath those rugged elms and yew tree's shade,  
Where heaves the turf in many a mouldering heap,  
Each in his narrow cell forever laid,  
The rude forefathers of the hamlet sleep."  

By far the most interesting and beautiful grave marker is one concerning Martin Power. This is situated in the centre of the graveyard and is constructed from Killaloe slate. It bears a remarkable resemblance to several that have recently been uncovered in Castlelough graveyard on the opposite side of the lake. It shows St. Michael with his trumpet, a skeleton with face of a bird holding the scales of justice, two doves with olive branches, two angles, two roosters and two baskets of flowers. Christ is depicted on the cross and by His side can be seen nails, pincers and hammer.

A local man named Martin Power, who was a soldier in the British army, was so filled with remorse after the death of his son, aged 22, that he refused to return to his regiment and became a deserter. He constructed this magnificent marker while on-the-run. It is said locally that he constructed it on Hobart Island in Church Bay.

"Here rests his head upon the lap of earth  
A youth to fortune and to fame unknown,  
Fair science frown'd not on his humble birth,  
And melancholy marked him for her own".
