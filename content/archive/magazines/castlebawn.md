**CASTLEBAWN**
Written by Pat Cody

Nestling on a small Island on Lough Derg, close to the shoreline in the townland of Carroweena, Ogonnelloe is the ruin of a 16th century Towerhouse, known as Castlebawn. It stands on a remote Island, shrouded by many years of ivy growth, hiding the history of its former years. In its day this Castle and Island would not have seemed so inaccessible, as Lough Derg and the River Shannon were effectively the motorway of 16th century Ireland, where goods and commerce busily plied up and down to the various towns and villages. Castlebawn was built at a strategic location overseeing goings-on in and out of the town of Scarriff with commanding views across Lough Derg. In the 16th century when Castlebawn was built, it was just one of a network of at least eleven lookout posts in the form of Towerhouses, dotted along the water's edge around Lough Derg alone. They included:- Portumna Castle, Castle Biggs, Oldcourt Castle, Cloonagoff Castle, Druminagh Castle, Knigh Castle, Dromineer Castle, Garrykennedy Castle, Castlelough, Derrycastle and Castlebawn.

Castlebawn was built by the McNamara chieftains during the mid to late 16th century. The family name of McNamara is still quite numerous around county Clare today. Records show that Castlebawn was built by Owen McNamara (1), but does not give the year of construction. The earliest date on record mentioning Castlebawn is 1570, when Sean McNamara, Chief of eastern Clann-Cuilein, died leaving Castlebawn to his son, Sir John McNamara (2). It passed through several generations of McNamara's until the year 1641 when it became the property of the Earl of Ossory (3). There is no evidence to suggest that the Earl of Ossory ever stayed there, only that he became the owner. Another reference to Castlebawn dates from 1669 when it, along with the whole parish of Ogonelloe, became the property of Captain Purdon (4). Likewise it is not recorded if Captain Purdon ever lived at Castlebawn. The last occupier of Castlebawn was Simon Flannery, earlier this century. Even today many local people still refer to it as Simon's Castle.

Sadly, Castlebawn's claim to fame, is in its unorthodox destruction by Government forces in 1827. Castlebawn was used as a Poteen distillery, which irritated the Authorities, as no revenue or taxes could be levied on the illicit brew. Its location being on an island, made it possible to keep a good lookout for any raiding parties of law enforcers, giving the occupants enough time to make their escape. A written description of these events was collected by the Folklore Commission of 1937/8, and goes as follows:— "*In 1827 it (the castle) was the scene of a dramatic siege. At that time it was the property of Major Purdon, and had long been a place of illicit spirit manufacture. Several efforts by the law had proved useless in halting the distillery. However, things were now at a head and the law were determined to put an end to the unlawful spirits. Cannon had previously been tried and it was decided that the only way to clear out the men inside was to surround the castle in boats and stay there until the distillers gave in. At first they were very determined and claimed that they would fight to the end, but eventually Major Purdon and the 93rd regiment, under Lieut. Sutherland from Killaloe, talked the illegal occupants into surrender. About a month later the castle was blown up by a Limerick regiment by order of the Government*" (5). An account of an earlier "siege" is given by Frenchman, d'Latocnaye, in his writings in 1796/7;

He states:— "*I followed the western course of Lough Derg, and on the way met an honest attorney going gaily to put the surrounding country under contribution. He pointed out to me, at some distance from the shore, a square tower situated on a rock. Some determined contrabandists had there established a distillery, with intention to pay no duties. They barricaded the place, and being provided with firearms, no custom officer dare hazard his life in approaching these friends of the "creation". To dislodge them, it was necessary to send troops with cannon, but the distance from the bank being considerable, and there being also a wish not to proceed to extremes, they proceeded to starve out the illicit distillers, who did not surrender until the fifteenth day, and then only after having effected an honourable capitulation*" (6).

Owing to this shortsighted remedy to the illegal distillery, one wall, the south wall, was completely destroyed, bringing with it the roof and top portion of the castle. Attempts to destroy the north wall failed, only managing to blow cavities in the wall, without toppling it. The result is a three walled castle, looking like a medieval handball alley, sheltered by a vaulted roof.

Castlebawn is unusual in plan from other towerhouses built during the latter half of the 16th century. The front door is not on ground level as one might expect, but is on the first floor, where an attacker might find it a little more difficult to gain access, which was probably a sign of the times. The three remaining walls have no evidence of having windows on the ground floor, so one can assume that no window existed in the south wall. This would have made the ground floor very dark leaving it only useful as a store.

On entering the front door opening on the first floor level, there is a small porter's lodge to one's right. This small room only measures 1.8m x 1.35m and was used as quarters for the sentry. It has a small loop (window) in the east wall. Overhead the entrance is the remains of the murder hole, so called as it allowed missiles to be thrown down on intruders. To the left of the front door are several steps leading up, possibly into a spiral staircase, now demolished. This first floor contains a narrow loop (window) in a squared embrasure in the north and west walls. Also in the west wall is an impressive stone mantled fireplace with a very large flue going up through the wall. This fireplace and its flue are made from limestone, differing from the rest of the castle as it is made from sandstone. The only other limestone used in the construction, is the front door and two internal doors. This first floor room has evidence of having a balcony or gallery overhead, as there are holes in the walls which would have supported timber beams. This room is covered over with a groin-vaulted ceiling, which differs in construction from other towerhouses, in that the vault was formed using planks of timber rather than the traditional wickerwork frame, the pattern of the planking being evident. Above the front door and the porter's lodge is another small room, which would have contained a trap door covering the murder hole. There is a narrow loop (window) in the north wall of this room but there are no other openings to the outside, which would discount it as being a garderobe (w.c.). Its purpose we can only guess at. Maybe it was used as an oratory, though having a murder hole in an oratory would seem inappropriate! This small room was joined to the spiral stairs by a short flight of steps leading up.

The second floor consists of a single room which has a large opening in the north wall. This probably housed a large stone carved mullioned window, affording it good views across Lough Derg. This room also contains a small circular aumbre (cupboard) in the southwest corner, which may have something to do with the chimney, as the flue from the first floor fireplace, is less than two metres away. This level also has evidence of vaulting as there are springers, probably rising to support a perimeter of vaults, giving the upper walls enough thickness to support a wallwalk around the roof of the castle. There is a stone corbel with a face carved on it, which probably belonged to this room, where it might have been used to support a roof truss.

The Island that remains today is quite small, being only about one tenth of an acre. Of course prior to the building of the hydroelectric powerstation at Ardnacrusha, and the subsequent raising of the water level on Lough Derg, by about four feet, it was a much larger Island.
