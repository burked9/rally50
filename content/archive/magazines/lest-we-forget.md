**"Lest We Forget"**
**THE FAMINE MEMORIAL PARK IN TUAMGRANEY, Co. CLARE**

Tuamgraney, a village with a population of less than 500, has the unique distinction of having two Memorial Parks within 500 metres of each other. On 20th July 1952, a Memorial Park on the 'Rock' of Tuamgraney was officially opened to the memory of the men and women of the East Clare Brigade I.R.A. 1916-1921. It preserved from the shades of oblivion our historic struggle for liberation. On 20th July 1997, the darkest hour in Ireland's history 'Black 47', when hunger and death stalked the land was remembered when Fr. Tom O Caoimh of the Heritage Council officially opened another Memorial Park.

This unique Park has been created on a one acre site outside the village of Tuamgraney, where it is estimated 7,000 of our ancestors were unceremoniously interred during those terrible years. The 'Pits' are still clearly visible and serve as a stark reminder of what happened here. A contemporary report published in the Limerick Chronicle on 6th January 1847, states "The Workhouse of Scariff, County Clare is so over-crowded with paupers, that a disease, almost amounting to a plague has brokenout amongst its inmates — the deaths averaging from four to twelve daily. It is horrifying to behold a donkey cart laden with five and six bodies, piled over each other, going to be interred and not a person attending the wretched cortege except the driver. The graves are so dug that the coffins are barely covered with earth, rendering the air infected. No coroner's inquest have been held".

The Scariff Workhouse Union, an area of 170 sq. miles with a population of 47,894, was declared in July 1839. Its Workhouse was built in 1841. The Union comprised of Scariff, Ogonnelloe, Killaloe, Bodyke, Tulla, Feakle, Whitegate, Mountshannon and Woodford. Scariff Workhouse was destroyed by fire on 9th June 1921, and all its records perished. Little remains of the Workhouse, which was so symptomatic of the tragedy, heartache and despair that was Ireland in the 1840s. Two gate-houses do exist and one of those is appropriately named the Coffin House.

The burial site consisting of one acre became known as the Casaoireach and there are two plausible explanations for this name:

1. In old Irish, the word Casaoireach traditionally referred to a badly tilled or uneven garden. The uneven surface resulting from the burial trenches which have subsided suggested the name.
2. The number of internees greatly outnumbered this supply of coffins. This led to the idea of rigging the coffin was a hinged bottom. When the coffin was lowered into the grave, a lever was pulled which opened and released the body. The word Casaoireach in this instance is a corruption of three Irish words i.e. Cath-siar-iad, which means Throw them back.

After the famine the graveyard continued to be used on a regular basis for inmates of the Workhouse. One burial here in the late 1930s speaks volumes about the state of society in rural Ireland. Michael Mullins was one of the hundreds of homeless boys and girls annually 'farmed out' to medium and large scale farmers. Only two conditions were strictly imposed — they had to attend school and they had to be brought up Catholics. Michael died on 10th June 1939 aged 18 years from a burst appendix. He was brought to the Casaoireach on a horse's cart and unceremoniously interred there. Michael's grave is now marked with a simple memorial stone.

Members of the Travelling Community were regularly interred here up to recent times. Here and there throughout the graveyard can be seen single unmarked stones which marks their last resting place. The one memorial in the graveyard with a name, is a simple iron cross which marks the grave of Mary Kerrigan who died in 1958.

A pair of old Cypress trees on the site which seem to be embracing each other was the symbolism that encouraged us to plant the site with trees. What better way is there to remember the forgotten and unfortunate victims of the last dark age in Irish history, than with the living gift of trees. Over twenty varieties of trees and shrubs have been planted. They mainly represent the indigenous trees and shrubs of County Clare and were identified from the Irish spelling of townlands in the County. There are at least 118 townlands in County Clare named after trees and shrubs. The most common tree is the Oak, whose Irish name 'Dair' is the root of 35 townlands. Oaks have been planted around the perimeter of the site. In time these will form what the botanists call the main storey. Beneath these will be the under storey, consisting of trees like Hazel, Hawthorn and Holly. The shrub layer is comprised of woody shrubs which are too shaded to grow tall and beneath these is the dwarf shrub layer consisting of Brambles and Heather. A cruciform shaped grassed area has been created through the centre with Mountain Ash or Rowan. The graveyard is now a sanctuary for wild plants, insects, birds and animals. It is therefore a living churchyard and the integrity of the site is protected. We hope that the project shows respect for death and reverence for life in all its forms.

The magnificent millstone which now serves as a memorial is unique. It was never used. It is one of a pair that were made for a corn mill at Scariff which accidentally burned down in 1868. It was stuck by lightening. In 1839, John O'Donovan when working for the Ordnance Survey wrote that the mill was built about 100 years ago. It was a Corn, Flour, Oil, Bark and Tuck mill and was seven stories high. It was a remarkable building as a road led to the seventh floor.

The millstone weighs three tons, is 46 centimetres in width and has a diameter of 1.8 metres. One side is chiselled smooth but not yet dressed for grinding corn. This was the most specialised job in the mill.

We should never be allowed to forget the misfortunes which befell our ancestors, whose destinies were pre-determined by outside influences. Fr. Thomas O Caoimh in his opening address stated "*The shadow cast by the Famine, An Gorta Mor, is a shadow that touches all our own family stories as we gather in our own places. When we gather in the famine places, at a famine graveyard, at a Workhouse, at a deserted village, we are reminded that this calamity really happened and that it was to our people that it happened. We don't have to reach back very far in the generations to meet our own who lived, died and found themselves on a strange shore through these terrible years. It's important for us to remember this not so as to foster bitterness or resentment to the world and particularly to the brother or sister in need at times of crisis all over the world. We've been there, we know, it's our story. Places like this park are 'heart-places' for any community. I hope many people will come here and will discover their own story a little more, will know themselves a little better and will find here the challenge to be creators of a better world*".
