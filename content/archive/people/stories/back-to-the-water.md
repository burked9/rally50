*Published in Derg Rally 24 Magazine (1999) by Brian J Goggin.*

**Back To The Water**

**M**any years ago, when the world was young, my wife Anne's parents built a holiday cottage near Kilgarvan. We lived in Dublin at the time and started going to Kilgarvan at weekends. Anne's family had some boats, but eventually we decided we needed one of our own; we bought a 14-foot Mirror sailing dinghy and (more or less) worked out how to drive it.

One August day, we were surprised to find the harbour filling up with boats. We learned that this was a rally of something called IWAI; someone - probably Maureen Duffy - persuaded us to join the IWAI and to enter our Mirror on the rally, even though we couldn't really participate properly.

The following year (or was it? Old age mixes memories), we borrowed Anne's father's 22-foot O'Day sailing cruiser and went on the rally ourselves. And the year after that, we bought a Privateer bermudan cutter, 17 foot on the waterline, which we called *Pynta Pleyne* because it was black with a cream top. Fr Flannery lent us an outboard and we went rallying again.

We did that for several years, even venturing on occasion to the pirate-infested waters of Lough Ree and Carrick-on-Shannon, where strange tribes of mariners conduct human sacrifices at midnight. Nick Coady sold us a Puffin dinghy for £50; we could tow it, row it and - after a fashion - sail it. Usually sideways.

With a draft of less than 18 inches with the plate up, we could get *Pynta Pleyne* into remote places; in light airs, one of us could nip over the side for a swim while the other sailed; we worked out the best way of storing wine to act as ballast. And we perfected the art of sailing on the river stretches; we could drop our mast temporarily for going under bridges and hoist it on the far side without losing way.

But on one rally, on Lough Ree, a strange illness called morning sickness struck half the crew, who had to clamber across seventeen boats (being smallest, we were always on the outside) in the early morning to find a quiet place, while the other half made tea and dry toast. The following year, the crew had increased by one - and it turned out that small sailing boats, needing two to sail them, are not ideal for babies. When babies grow into young children, they can get quite bored during long voyages (anything more than fifteen minutes), so gradually we used our boats less and less. After our daughter Carolan was joined, four years later, by Ian, we hauled the boat out and left it in the shed at Kilgarvan.

Eventually we sold it, using the proceeds to buy a Skipper dinghy, which we could trail, launch and sail relatively quickly, and an Optimist in which Carolan could learn to sail. Later we bought a windsurfer and a small dinghy for Ian. But we had nothing we could sleep on; we gave up rallying, IWAI meetings and other watery activities, except for one holiday on an Emerald Star boat on the Shannon-Erne Waterway. That apart, we forgot about the waterways altogether until a friend told us she was looking for a good home for a cruiser. We were tempted - and we fell.

We bought the boat at the end of the season in 1996 and Teddy Knight agreed to accommodate it at Shannon Sailing in Dromineer. We renamed it *carian@iol.ie* (which is one of our email addresses) and started trying to learn about the ownership, maintenance and running of large boats with diesel engines. In March 1997 we returned to the water, making the first of several trips to Dromaan. And later that year we went on the Lough Derg Rally, for the first time in more than ten years; and the rain moderated, our offspring enjoyed themselves so much that we have no option but to go again this year.

So what has changed while we've been away? The first thing that struck us was the increase in the number and size of the boats, on Lough Derg in particular. (I remember one night in Mountshannon, trying to guess just how many millions of pounds worth of boat were tied up in the harbour). The boats are more powerful, with many more gadgets. Their shape has changed: they're taller, less boat-like; their designs driven by the need to provide bathing platforms, sun-decks, showers and television lounges - but in my view they have less character than do more traditional designs. And they're much, much bigger: our 34-footer would have been a big boat in the past, but now it's really quite small.

There are lots more toys around - and I'm sorry to say that many of them are noisy nuisances, especially the jet-skies and the fast speedboats towing yellow bananas. I don't mind if they keep away, far away, from where I am, but I dislike their noise and I particularly dislike being rocked by boats zooming past, on the lake or on the river. I hope that the prat who zoomed upriver, under Portumna Bridge, at nine o'clock one night, and zoomed back down at about eleven, will roast in hell. And I'm seriously thinking of learning how to use a camera so that I can take photographs of speeders and report them to the authorities.

There are more facilities. The generous taxpayers of Ireland and Europe have built harbours, toilets, showers and so forth, although I don't think the supply can possibly keep up with the demand. The country is much richer than it was, and people keep buying boats; the potential demand for harbour space, like that for any free good, is infinite.

I would therefore support the introduction of either harbour charges or an annual registration fee; I don't see why the taxpayer should continue subsidising the leisure pursuits of the better-off .....

.... especially when people are willing and able to pay for other goods and services, which are now much more widely available. Shops carry a wider range of products, and especially of food; there are more, and better, restaurants; above all, there are marinas and other mooring facilities all over the place. The Shannon is providing a living for more and more people.

Things are also becoming more regulated. That's inevitable with increasing use - and with the results of previous freedom now becoming apparent. Speed limits, pump-out stations, the EU recreational craft regulations and the new boat-handling certification scheme all represent increasing regulation, complexity and formality. Inevitable, but still: there are drawbacks.

When I'm feeling pessimistic, the Shannon now reminds me of Legoland. You sit in a boat equipped with a steering wheel, and you travel past one sight after another. You may think you're steering, but you're actually travelling along a predetermined route. The Shannon could get like that: a sort of floating caravan park with all mod cons in the mobile homes and with a safe, managed progression from one fully-serviced site to another.

But then you find your rope being taken by another boater, when a chat leads to a cup of coffee or a drink. Or you watch birds in the Carnadoe Waters or listen to the birdsong up the Woodford River. You see the kids keeping themselves amused for hours on end at a rally, making friends they'll meet again on the river. You meet one of the barges or the other more interesting boats. And you know that, despite all the change, many things - the best things - are still the same.

Brian J Goggin
