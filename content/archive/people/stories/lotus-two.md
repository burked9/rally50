Title: "Lotus Two" to Lough Erne or Shingles on the Shannon
Date: 1996-01-01
Category: People
Slug: archive/people/stories/lotus-two

*This story by Brendan McMahon was published in the Derg Rally 21 Magazine (1996).*

"Lotus Two" has been cruised so often to Lough Key that she almost knows her own way. In June 1995 she cruised Upper Lough Erne to Ballinaleck, Enniskillen. Skipper was Tom Duffy and crew Brendan McMahon. As befits a boat owned by Tom and Maureen, each a former Commodore of the Lough Derg Rally, she was most meticulously prepared, and so gave no trouble of any kind on this 317 mile trip. She had been well supplied with essential supplies, and there was some food too!

**THURSDAY, 8th JUNE:** leaving home in Limerick at 8.00 am, we got under way from Kilgarvan at 9.27, and made Portumna in good time for the 11 o'clock bridge opening. Tom chose to pass through Athlone and make for Hodson's Bay. He had heard of a new quay there, but we had no pilotage details. Approaching with prudence, we spotted a quay, and found our way with ease into a lovely new well-sheltered harbour. This was new cruising ground for crew, whose last trip up-river past Portumna had been in 1932, en route to Dun Laoghaire via the Grand Canal and Ringsend Dock. With boyhood memories of a loving mother's admonition — "Eat up now, you do not know where your next meal is coming from", he now suggested dinner in the hotel to celebrate our being on our way. Skipper was to learn that crew was most inventive when it came to finding reasons to celebrate. Our meal was superb, but perhaps over elaborate for us, and we later decided that simpler fare suited our aged digestions better, and we did not repeat this excess.

**FRIDAY, 9th JUNE:** rather cold and grey with a Northerly breeze to put a little sea on Lough Ree, saw our start enlivened by the arrival of a helicopter to take some golfers from the hotel. Off by 9.25, once safely out in the main channel, Skipper retired below for a rest. Crew, whose most recent navigational experiences have been offshore, revelled in being (a) under power and so able to proceed dead up-wind, (b) under a hood, protected by a windscreen with an electric wiper, and (c) able to follow conveniently numbered buoys. His only problem was that an itch, first noticed going ashore last evening, still persisted on his left side.

Skipper soon revived and we shared the helm in one hour spells. Crew greatly enjoyed the scenery, curious at the "Coarse Anglers" under their umbrellas, and the Sun came out to show the wooded shores of L. Bofin & L. Boderg to perfection. The Keeper at Victoria Lock owns "Lotus One", leading to some chat. We found him and all the O.P.W. staff very nice and friendly, polite, helpful and well turned out. 18.45 saw us berthed at Carrick Craft's fine marina at Carrick-on-Shannon. Skipper still felt rather over-fed from last night, but crew strolled around Carrick, nosed out Cryan's and greatly enjoyed a couple of pints, followed by delightful grilled salmon, chips and salad for £9.00. Then followed what was to become a routine — a walk for petrol. Our 1.6 L. "Ford" petrol is lovely and smooth, but rather thirsty. This was another "Eye Opener" for crew, whose own auxiliary power comes from a Volvo Penta MD1 which consumes about 10 gallons of duty free diesel per season.

**SATURDAY, 10th JUNE:** We had to wait a little until the "Emerald Star" offices opened at 9.30 to buy lock cards, but were rewarded to find a most attractive, well-groomed and uniformed young lady to sell them — even a little thing like that helps! Again, we were off by 9.28, up-river to turn "Right Hand Down" into the Leitrim River, past Leitrim Village to Lock 16. Here was another friendly keeper to explain that our "Smart Card" bought us for 50p, twenty minutes of the hydraulic pump which operates the lock. It is "Mechanised" rather than "Automatic". One has to push the buttons to operate it. Crew failed to pay adequate attention to the instructions. Leaving Lock 15, he must have opened the gates out before the levels were quite even, and tripped the pump. A call for help brought the keeper in 15 minutes, and crew studied what he should have first time, how to judge when the levels were right when the "Swirl" ceases. Also, while all buttons are marked "Press and Hold", that is only necessary for the gates. Once the sluices are opened the finger can be removed so one can go to the lock and look down to see that all is in order. Our next lesson came at Lock 13. Having checked that boat and dinghy were safely in, crew operated the control to close the gates. From there, when the lock is at low level, one cannot see the boat. Only the expensive sound of breaking fibreglass told crew that the dinghy had blown back between the gates! A Gunn'le was crushed, but it still floated, and was not required during the cruise. Next, an impatient foreign lady went behind crew's back and pressed the "Open Gate" button while there was still a good 9" difference in the levels. This time the switch did not trip as it should have, and the resultant "Swirl" was spectacular. Crew was learning, and she was the last person to get near the control panel when our card was in it! We tended to call these "Germans" but many were Swiss and one crew Swedish.

Locks 11, 10 and 9 come together at Kilclare up into Dromaleague L. and L. Scur. Through Lock 8 and we were descending to stop at Lock 7 at Ballinamore for stores and petrol at 15.06. By 19.06 we were into Garadice Lake, most attractive and quite large, with wooded islands. Crew, whose business that had been, noticed Sitka Spruce plantations, and wondered at the economics of such on an island in view of the heavy extraction costs. 19.25 saw us moored at "Haughton's Shore Moorings" a most delightful and sheltered little harbour, in the middle of nowhere. Skipper and crew soon got down to another established routine. This involved lightening ship by imbibing good draughts of "Black Bush". Skipper had earlier explained to crew his partiality for this, and crew, ever obliging, had agreed that it would be bad for morale to let him drink alone. It is quite amazing what this does for the level of chat, even between two people who have been together on the boat all day. Crew thought that this would be a good time to break a bit of bad news. That itch, first noticed at Hodson's Bay, had not gone away. The presence of red humps on his left side suggested that he had Shingles. This was rather a damper, and made both less than enthusiastic when a young lady arrived with a leaflet inviting us to "The Mucky Duck" pub "600 metres from Haughton's Jetty — ring 049 33018 for free taxi" and offering "A game of cards, darts or pool, watch T.V., and taste some of the finest drink available". As it was Saturday night we could also "Dance the night away with the locals". We did establish that Mass was not practical for the morning. However, we dined well aboard off cold Turkey and Ham, boiled new potatoes and peas, and as both are a little chary of coffee upsetting sleep, milk and choc. bis. Crew, who likes exercise, walked a few miles to Newtowngore, being in the opposite direction to the "Mucky Duck". In spite of two Dispirin twice after the walk, he found little sleep. The noise from the heating plant on an adjacent hired cruiser did not help.

**SUNDAY, 11th JUNE:** After photographs of this lovely place, we got away once more, remarkably consistent in our timing, at 9.35. It was a lovely sunny morning, and we enjoyed having our hood down. By 11.25 we were through Ballyconnell, and 11.39 out of Lock 2, the last in the Republic of Ireland, once more greatly helped by the friendly keeper. What a contrast came with Lock 1! It is under the control of the N.I. Department of Agriculture, and the keeper was the only offensive and unfriendly person we met on our trip. Sadly, crew was not feeling well, not a bit tolerant, and decided to be equally offensive back. We were glad to be out of the lock and under Aghalane bridge No. 31 into the Woodford River and entered Upper Lough Erne 12.58. Almost at once, amidst a confusion of marks, it was "Right Hand Down" once more, or "Hard to Starboard" if you prefer it, into the River Erne, to moor at the fine new "Emerald Star" marina at Belturbet 13.55. Here, manager David Saunders kindly arranged a visit from their doctor. He cheerfully confirmed the good news — "It is only Shingles, it is no harm and is not infectious". While it not being infectious was good news indeed for Skipper, crew concluded that doctor had never had Shingles himself, and while wishing him no harm, felt that having them might improve his medical education. He prescribed treatment — a pain killer and some stuff to be painted on. Thus, for the rest of the cruise, skipper had to paint on this "Anti-Fouling" four times a day, and crew wondered if this was what having the pain killed was, what it would be like if it were alive. However, both were delighted that our cruise would continue, and crew had yet another good reason to celebrate. Skipper took crew and manager to a large "Steak, Onions and Chips" meal at a grill, after which he had to rest. Crew walked into Belturbet, for petrol, to enquire about evening Mass, none, and for the exercise. As it was quite a distance to carry the petrol, he got somewhat more than he felt strictly necessary, and decided that an essential piece of new equipment for "Lotus Two" would be a suitable trolley. He felt totally justified in, and skipper was restored enough to be able to enjoy, considerable "Black Bush". Then, still replete from that large, late lunch, crew was yet again spared from cooking as "High Tea", with cups, saucers and plates, not to mention napkins, was enjoyed with "Foxford Lunch".

**MONDAY, 12th JUNE:** We bought some stores, sent post cards, and got away a little later than usual at 10.15 on a superb morning. In thirty minutes we had passed under "Foalies Bridge" into Upper Lough Erne, and at once revelled in the most beautiful vistas of sun-drenched lake, wooded shores and islands, with a number of castles, old and new. It was only a short distance to our first berth at Crom Castle. The marina was free, but there was a charge for the "Visitors' Centre". This was well worthwhile, giving a history of this seat of Lord Erne, with a delightful account of social activities in the late nineteenth century and up to World War One. Much of the activity centred on the lake, with a lot of sailing, some steam-boating and lots of dinghies. Among the many fascinating old photographs was one of "May, Countess of Limerick", as an elderly lady all in black, with the most enormous hat. A page of the visitors' book bore her autograph, "May, Limerick" .... Both strolled to the Rhododendron gardens. Then skipper retired to rest, while crew walked to photograph both Old Crom Castle and the present Castle, and enjoy the quite sublime surroundings. It was a busy scene. Among the ruins of the old castle, a professional camera crew were recording the backing video for a singer. They made clear that the background sound did not matter. They were not recording sound. That had been done in a studio. The fact that the chap was singing his heart out did not matter, it was only to get his visual appearance right. Next time crew saw on T.V. somebody apparently singing lustily in a most precarious situation, he would know how it was done. Several groups of "Blue Peter" children were busy on a treasure hunt and studying nature. They impressed with their most obvious happiness, and good natured fun, their excellent manners and orderly behaviour, in neat navy uniforms. One knew one was in a foreign country.

Next we moved to Inishcorcaig. Here we found a little marina at a privately owned island with a small private house restaurant. There was nothing else to do or see and it was too early to eat so we moved on. Old cookers, broken fridges and odd gas cylinders were thrown about the place. We felt back in Ireland. The marks, which had been fine in confined waters, now proved inadequate, too few and too small. Crew wished for a chart with a compass rose and a steering compass, but got lost. Intending to cruise the Eastern shore, we found ourselves on the Western side. Now it became a matter of looking for a mark, closing with caution, reading the small numerals and trying, with the aid of a magnifying glass, (Lotus Two has everything!), to find that number on the map. Without hitting anything, we finally found our way into the downstream River Erne to "The Moorings", Ballinaleck, Enniskillen. This is a fine marina by a restaurant. Crew strolled to the village to shop, buy more local postcards to prove that we got here, again noticing the very tidy countryside and most "Un-Irish" quality of the road. It was fine enough to drink our "Black Bush" in the cockpit enjoying the surroundings. Crew again showed his skill at avoiding real cooking by serving cold turkey and ham, with new pops, but ringing the changes with beans. If this goes on, we are going to get very tired of cold turkey and ham. Later, we strolled to the restaurant bar and met the owners. There is no charge for the marina, but would be no toilet facilities open in the morning either. We slept well in such beautiful and peaceful surroundings. No doubt the satisfaction of having got here helped us to relax.

**TUESDAY, 13th JUNE:** As we planned a long day, and had been early to bunk, we left at 6.50 on a superb calm morning with a clear blue sky. Even at that hour, it was pleasantly warm with the hood down. We took a different channel to Carrybridge, where we felt lucky to find about the only free berth. Spotless public toilets ashore were welcomed. After breakfast, we shifted to the berth under the hotel, where there were several pumps, as it serves as a car filling station also — "Yes of course, you can have petrol, but you will have to wait while I call him. He is still in bed. It was late last night". The shop across the road would not open until 9.30. In the car park, dozing on a grassy bank while waiting, crew heard a car drive in and stop. Opening a lazy eye, he found himself under the scrutiny of two shirt sleeved young R.U.C. men. As he explained one commented with a laugh — "Just thought you might be drunk from the pub last night"! It is a salutary thought that only last year, it might have been a body.

It was great to have full, hose-filled fuel and water tanks. Lough Erne still looked superb, but we got lost again. Motoring happily on what crew thought our proper course, we met two large hired cruisers coming the opposite way, "Aren't they very friendly, waving so much — ". Then we heard cries in broken English — "Back! Back! All rocks up there". It took a few minutes to convince ourselves that they were right. Off we chased to find a mark and resume our proper course. One trusted us and followed, the other did not and disappeared off in the opposite direction. One of the problems on this big lake is the number of islands and bays. Many have private moorings, so the presence of a couple of boats does not mean good water or a channel.

We were a little sad to leave Lough Erne at 11.45, and re-enter the Woodford River. We caught up on an elderly "German" couple. He commanded. She ran about taking lines and operating the lock. They allowed us in after them. Strange panic and weird cries disclosed her trying to retrieve a lost fender. They were rather slow and we got ahead but felt obliged to wait for them at the next lock. Thus they only had to motor in. Delighted, she insisted on using her card. We explained that we would stop at Ballyconnell for lunch, at least we tried to. Petrol is convenient there. They did the same, so crew took their lines. They left when we did, and we worried if we would have to wait all day for them. Then we came to a lake, so we waved farewell and powered away, and they seemed to understand and be quite happy. At Lock 5 we came upon some young and very friendly Swiss. They waited for us and used their card, but contrary to instructions opened all sluices when filling the lock and poor "Lotus Two" was tossed about like a ping pong ball on a fountain. Both were tired when we reached Ballinamore — "Hurrah" for our "Mood Enhancer". Crew walked for petrol (!) and then did the nearest thing of the voyage to cooking, grilling some Ulster ham steaks and serving with his own invention "Marinated in Pineapple", i.e., when grilling four steaks, put the first two when done into a saucepan with the juice from the tin of Pineapple and leave on the cooker without the ring on. Put in the second pair while waiting for the pops to finish. It is really wonderful what a long happy day of achievement followed by a couple of large ones does for the enjoyment of food.

**WEDNESDAY, 14th June:** It was yet another glorious morning. How could we be so lucky? Of course, it was "The Summer of '95". Back to our "normal" time, we were away at 8.40. We had a delay at Lock 7, Ballyduff. There was no traffic light as we entered, and the control did not respond to our card. Crew was just about to try the intercom for help when skipper shouted "The Light has come on - try again". All was now in order and we were on our way. Next a beautiful blue Kingfisher came out from the bank to Starboard and flew straight ahead of us twice before returning to it. This was superb. By 10.08 we were out of Lock 8, Castlefore. It is downhill all the way home now. When we came to Lock 9, it seemed dead. Worse again, there was no response to the intercom. After about fifteen minutes, skipper saw a van stop on the road nearby and sought advice. They turned out to be electrical contractors working for the O.P.W. They opened the control cabinet, reset the overload and had us in action. Then along came Gerry McCabe of O.P.W., who knew the lock had been out of order. The remaining locks are quite close and he assured us that he would be about all day in case of trouble. In fact we had none. He was most friendly. By 13.16 we were out of the system and tied alongside at Leitrim village for lunch, and petrol. At 14.05 we departed. Now skipper showed his great kindness. Although so often to Lough key that he had no great interest in going there again and he would surely have preferred to get home, as crew had never been there, and had earlier expressed a desire to see it, skipper now turned "Lotus Two's" bow up river on reaching the Shannon. By 15.00 we had passed Coothall, with a couple of interesting houses on the Port Side (!) and at 15.30 entered Clarendon Lock astern of "Utopia" — family friends of skipper. A small boy at the helm impressed. By 15.42 we were out into Lough Key. It was still sunny, but a fresh breeze put up some seas and threw spray high over us. Crew was tickled pink to be cruising on this lovely lake, which had long been an ambition. We closed into Rockingham, but did not berth, instead turing for home. By 17.55 we had moored at our old berth at Carrick Craft marina. Skipper and crew had not experienced one disagreement so far on this cruise, and most certainly did not now, on the matter of "Black Bush". It did not take crew's inventiveness to point out that we had something to celebrate. Skipper went even further, joining crew in making a bee line for Cryans, and more Salmon Steaks, with Guinness and this time boiled new pops. In keeping with the mood, crew felt the walk across the bridge for petrol had got much shorter than on the way up.

**THURSDAY, 15th JUNE:** Our guide shows Albert Lock to open at 9.00, and so we were there expectantly at 8.55. No keeper appeared until 9.30. He redeemed himself in Skipper's eyes when the postman arrived and he got him to take Skipper's mail. At Roosky Lock we were the last of six boats and crew felt we would not fit. "Who says so?" asked the keeper, ordered him aboard, turned us diagonally, and we all did. At 12.15 we had a long delay at Tarmonbarry Lock, for no reason we saw except that as the lock was empty the keeper waited until some craft came up before he filled it. We were not that happy at it. As we had approached the jetty, two big hired cruisers left us little room. As skipper brought our bow close, crew hopped ashore, looped a bow line around a cleat, and ran aft to put the stern rope around another. All went well, and we berthed neatly. From the "Flying Bridge" of the nearest boat, amidst a lot of clatter in a foreign tongue, came a shout in good English — "How old are you?". Crew replied — "Oh, — you must not ask that. You may ask — "How young are you?". "I am only 71 and skipper is only 77". "You are so young came the retort". Crew felt this a compliment, and wondered what the chap would think if he knew about the Shingles! In the lock was a little 20' sailing yacht, outboard driven, mast on deck, with an elderly couple, and a U.K. Ensign. We had seen them earlier on the Restored canal and shouted "You are a long way from home". As he had photographed them, crew now got their name and address, "W. Watson, Riverside Cottage, West End, Dailly, Ayreshire". Later, letters were exchanged. They have done great cruising on this little yacht and had two lovely weeks sailing around Lough Ree. Crew again enjoyed L. Boderg and L. Bofin and through Lanesboro 14.02, skipper went below for a rest, leaving crew to greatly enjoy buoy hopping down Ree and past Hodson's Bay once more, said to be the centre of Ireland. We found Athlone Lock open with three boats already in, so it closed behind us and after skipper had some advantageous chat with his friend keeper Percy Hewitt we were out by 16.48. Clonmacnoise looked much better in this warm evening sunshine than under the cold and grey northerlies on our way up. A pair of swans had a clutch of really tiny cygnets, some perched on Mother's back — delightful. After a stop for petrol at Shannonbridge we berthed at the "Silverline" marina at Banagher. Guess what came then? After that, crew sneaked a nasty one on patient skipper, serving a couple cans of "Chicken Stew". This is another old trick of his, but usually comes re-inforced with extra chicken. The great thing about cruising at this time of year is that new "pops" are lovely. Skipper and crew shared this liking, which did something to compensate for the lack of "Real" cooking. Then both strolled ashore, as skipper's mother was a Kenny from here, and her builder family customers of crew!

**FRIDAY, 16th June:** According to our guide, Meelick Lock is also scheduled to open at 9.00 am, and we left to be there in good time. In fact, there was another long, unexplained delay, and we did not get through until 10.22, the first of seven craft. As a result we were just late for the 11 o'clock opening of Portumna bridge and had another long delay there, only finally getting away at 12.35. It was a nasty day on Lough Derg, overcast, grey and windy, ideal for homecoming, and we moored at our home berth at Kilgarvan at 13.40. Skipper's wife, Maureen, and daughter Natalie were there with the car for us. First it was "Tidy Ship" and load car. Tom Corry had also come over to welcome him home, and now followed one of those delightful occasions, all the more enjoyable because they are unexpected. Skipper and crew have long past the age when they can take "Strong Drink" early in the day without suffering the consequences. Both are almost too responsible. It was far too early in the day. Both Maureen and Natalie "had homes to go to". However, what we all did was to retire to Tom Corry's lovely and immaculate "Freeman" 27', with a full bottle of "Black Bush". The best parties are made up from old friends with one or two who had not met before, and so this turned out. As we all know, "A Bird Does Not Fly On One Wing". Also "Who Ever Saw One Without Tail Feathers?". Then it was agreed that to leave drink aboard might put unreasonable temptation to any younger people who might be aboard later. Finally, the ship provided a fine fruit cake, which served as lunch, and we had a non-drinking Duffy to drive us home. Skipper and crew agreed a fitting end to a fine cruise, admirably planned and executed, Deo Gratias.

**Brendan McMahon**
