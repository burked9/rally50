Rally Magazine 23 (1998): The Fourth Rock

Published in Rally 23 Magazine by John Moore.

THE FOURTH ROCK

My first recollection of "The Shannon" is as a four year old watching the older children swimming and diving at Skehanna on lovely sunny days. My Grandmother, Mary Maher, would take my sister, all the neighbours children and me to the lake day after day and sit and read her newspaper while we played and swam in a great swarm. There were often upwards of seventeen children ranging in age from babes in arms to young teenagers. My Grandmother seems to totally ignore us and showed little or no sign of worry or concern at our antics which was very unusual in that area as most of the adults had an irrational fear of "The Shannon".

With hindsight I now realise that we were much safer than we appeared to be because as a group we were self-policing and there was an in-built pecking order that at the time was taken for granted.

At Cleary's Quay there are a series of four rocks, the first of which is visible over the water and quite close to the shore. The older children would never allow the younger ones beyond the "first rock". From the shore to here the bottom of the lake is very rocky and this kept the smaller children in check because it was such an effort for them to make their way out. The effort of navigating their way took their minds off swimming and before they knew it they were comfortable in the water but frustrated by the rocks and anxious to get to that lovely sandy bottom beyond. The older children who had by now progressed to the "second rock" seemed to have an intuitive feeling when a child was ready to progress and would take them on their backs or in their arms on short trips into the promised land. And so over the long hot summers of schooldays all the local children got their water wings.

Once a child made it to that lovely sandy spot beyond first base it was seldom little more than days before you would hear squeals of delight as finally, like a bird leaving the nest for its maiden flight, he or she declared to the swarm "I can swim, I can swim". This declaration in itself was never enough to be given the freedom to swim to the "second rock" for the entire swarm would gather around and with overwater and underwater observers the fledgling would be put through its paces to make sure there was no cheating or false claims.

When the assembled jury was satisfied that the fledgling could indeed swim there followed a mass eruption of joy and excitement that even now, for almost forty years, I can remember as vividly as if it were yesterday. I can also remember the utter devastation on the faces of those who were caught using the tips of their toes to kick themselves off the soft clean sand like ballet dancers making enormous leaps across the stage, and who were sent packing back to the nursery with their tails between their legs!

After a period in Limbo where a child could now swim but was still confined to the relative safety of the "second rock" the ones showing promise were often encouraged by the older ones to have a go at the "third rock". This could often take a couple of days of cajoling, threatening and begging before the victim would embark on this voyage into the unknown. The main problem with getting to the "third rock" was locating it. It was in about four and a half feet of water and while swimming you couldn't see it, so it was a do or die event. Many victims had to be rescued by the jury and taken back to the shallows to gain more experience and courage, for courage was the main ingredient needed to take on that task.

The "fourth rock" was a killer, situated quite a distance from the "third" and in about nine feet of water with its pointy tip hidden below the surface and situated about twenty feet off the point of the main pier. It was a feat of endurance and navigation that would put the fear of God into Columbus himself and was an event of enormous importance to everybody in the swarm. The very fact that you were prepared to undergo this dangerous initiation, in itself, gave you great credibility and social position regardless of success or failure.

When at last you made it to the "fourth rock" you became an elder statesman or woman or should I say "person" and your responsibility and duty to the swarm became even greater. You were then expected to be ever vigilant and even to take one or two of the little ones under your wing and guide them on their journey to the promised land of the "fourth rock". From there we ventured on great voyages out onto the lake watched in awe by the fledglings from their watery nest and returned like conquering heroes with tales of other unnamed and uncharted rocks. On these great voyages we saw in the distance the gleaming white cruisers on the Steamers Course and dreamed that one day we too would have such a water chariot and embark on journeys beyond our wildest dreams. My first such journey was Rally 18!

Over those nine or ten years in the early sixties there was never an accident or a drowning among the swarm, and that despite the fact that hardly a single adult in the area could swim or were ever present on Sundays to throw pennies into the crystal clear water for us to dive for. Maybe if we could bring that same sense of responsibility and caring that was present in the swarm into adulthood, then the Shannon and life and general would be a much safer and magical place.

by John Moore
